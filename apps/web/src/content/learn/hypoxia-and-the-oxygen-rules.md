Hypoxia does not hurt. That is the problem. The first symptoms the Pilot's Handbook lists include euphoria and a carefree feeling, which is about the worst thing a brain can feel while it is getting less capable.

So the oxygen rules are not a judgment call made in the moment. They are altitudes and minutes, decided on the ground, before your judgment is the thing being affected.

## What is happening

At altitude the air still has the same share of oxygen, about 21%, but there is less air. Lower pressure means each breath carries fewer oxygen molecules into the lungs. The blood picks up less, and the brain, which uses a lot of it, feels it first.

The handbook names four kinds of hypoxia. The one altitude causes is hypoxic hypoxia: not enough oxygen gets into the blood in the first place. The others come from blood that cannot carry it (carbon monoxide does this), blood that is not moving well, and cells that cannot use the oxygen they get. Alcohol is in that last group, and the handbook notes one ounce of it can act like an extra 2,000 ft of altitude.

The symptoms vary by person: blue lips and fingernails, headache, slower reactions, poor judgment, tunnel vision. Many pilots learn their own early signs in an altitude chamber, which is the reason the FAA runs a physiology course.

## How much time you have

The time of useful consciousness is how long you can still take the right action, like putting on a mask or starting a descent, after your oxygen supply drops. It is not the time until you pass out. The advisory circular is blunt: impaired performance may be immediate.

Here are some rows from AC 61-107B Figure 2-3:

- **18,000 ft**: 20 to 30 minutes
- **25,000 ft**: 3 to 5 minutes, or 1.5 to 2.5 minutes after a rapid decompression
- **30,000 ft**: 1 to 2 minutes
- **35,000 ft**: 30 seconds to 1 minute
- **40,000 ft**: 15 to 20 seconds

A caution was added under the table in 2015. The times are averages, and people vary widely. The circular's advice is to assume the lower number is the one that limits you.

The drop is steep. Going from 25,000 to 35,000 ft takes your time from minutes to seconds. And a slow decompression, the circular warns, can be more dangerous than a fast one, because nothing loud tells you it is happening.

## When the rules require oxygen

For an airplane without pressurization, 91.211(a) sets three thresholds by cabin pressure altitude:

- **Above 12,500 ft up to 14,000 ft MSL**: the required minimum flight crew uses oxygen for the part of the flight at those altitudes that lasts more than 30 minutes.
- **Above 14,000 ft MSL**: the required crew uses oxygen the entire time.
- **Above 15,000 ft MSL**: every occupant must be provided with oxygen.

Note the words. Above 15,000 ft, passengers must be *provided* oxygen; they are not required to use it. The crew is required to use it.

For pressurized airplanes, 91.211(b) adds rules by flight level. Above FL250, there must be at least a 10-minute supply for each occupant for a descent if pressurization fails. Above FL350, one pilot at the controls wears a sealed mask, with a quick-donning mask exception that ends at FL410.

## A worked example

You plan a cross-country at 13,500 ft in an airplane without pressurization. The leg spends 45 minutes at that altitude.

- Cabin altitude: 13,500 ft, inside the 12,500 to 14,000 ft band
- Rule: 91.211(a)(1)
- Time in the band: 45 minutes
- Time over 30 minutes: **15 minutes on oxygen for the crew**

Climb to 14,500 ft and the answer changes: now the crew uses oxygen the whole time at that altitude, not just past 30 minutes.

The rule is a floor, not a target. Hypoxia can start lower, and the symptoms differ from one person to the next. A pulse oximeter, which the Pilot's Handbook describes in chapter 7, and an early start on oxygen cost little.
