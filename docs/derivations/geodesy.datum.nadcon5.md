# NADCON5 grid transformation (`geodesy.datum.nadcon5`)

## Method

A continental datum is not wrong by a constant. NAD 27 was built from a survey that grew outward from one station over decades, and the amount it differs from NAD 83 changes from place to place — tens of meters in Colorado, a few in Illinois — in a pattern no single set of parameters can carry. The National Geodetic Survey published that pattern as grids of latitude and longitude shifts, and this tool reads them: the point is located in the grid for its region, the shift at that point is interpolated from the surrounding nodes by the biquadratic scheme NGS uses, and the shift is added. The reverse direction has no closed form, because the shift to apply depends on the answer, so it is iterated until it settles.

The grids and how NGS applies them are documented in NOAA Technical Report NOS NGS 63 (Smith and Bilich, 2017, revised 2020). The interpolation between grid nodes is documented in NOAA Technical Memorandum NOS NGS 84, *Biquadratic Interpolation* (Smith, 2022).

## Equations

- Forward: φ′ = φ + Δφ(φ, λ), λ′ = λ + Δλ(φ, λ), the shifts interpolated from the region's grid.
- Interpolation: NGS's `qterp` biquadratic over the nine nodes around the point, quadratic in each direction rather than bilinear (NOAA TM NOS NGS 84, section 2).
- Reverse: iterate φ ← φ′ − Δφ(φ, λ), λ ← λ′ − Δλ(φ, λ) from the target as the first guess, until the move stops.
- The reported shift is the geodesic distance between the two positions, with its azimuth, and the components as `dlat` and `dlon`.

## Symbols and units

φ and λ are latitude and longitude in degrees; Δφ and Δλ the grid shifts, stored in arcseconds and reported as `dlat` and `dlon`; the shift is a distance in meters. The grids are the NADCON5 first-step files, carried as an on-demand data asset of about 1.8 MB, checked by digest before use.

## Domain

Seven regions, each with its own grid and its own source datum: NAD 27 in the conterminous United States and in Alaska, Old Hawaiian, Puerto Rico 1940 for Puerto Rico and the Virgin Islands, St. Paul 1952, American Samoa 1962, and Guam 1963 for Guam and the Northern Marianas. A point outside every grid is refused, naming the regions, rather than extrapolated — a grid has nothing to say beyond its edge, and the regional Helmert transformation is the fallback for anywhere else.

## Approximations

The interpolation between grid nodes, which is NGS's own scheme and is what NGS means by the transformation, so it is not a departure from the reference. The real limit is the grid itself: NGS states this first step at the decimeter level, about 0.15 m at one sigma for NAD 27 in the conterminous states. That is far coarser than anything the arithmetic contributes, and it is what the answer is worth.

## Worked example

- sourcePublisher: PROJ contributors, applying grids published by the National Geodetic Survey
- sourceTitle: PROJ `+proj=gridshift`, through pyproj
- sourceEdition: PROJ 9.3, NADCON5 grids dated 20160901
- sourceLocator: `us_noaa_nadcon5_nad27_nad83_1986_conus.tif` at 39.224, −98.542, near Meades Ranch, the NAD 27 origin
- independent: yes
- inputs: NAD 27, latitude 39.224, longitude −98.542
- outputs: latitude 39.224009813855886, longitude −98.542345044318
- tolerance: 1e-9°, about 0.1 mm, seven orders inside the decimeter NGS states
- verifiedBy: golden vector v001, run by the core on every build
- verifiedOn: 2026-09-23

The reference is PROJ reading the same NGS grid files: 34 vectors across all seven regions, generated by driving `+proj=gridshift` through pyproj. That is a different implementation of the interpolation over identical source data, which is the right comparison for a grid transformation — the question is whether the nodes are being found and weighted correctly, not whether the grid is right.

A second check comes from inside the catalog and is worth having because it is sensitive to something else entirely. At this point the grid moves the position 29.815 m; the single-parameter EPSG Helmert in `geodesy.datum.legacy` moves it 31.793 m. The 2.5 m between them is exactly the quantity a grid exists to carry and a Helmert cannot, and it varies with place — 3.1 m in Denver, 3.6 m in Chicago, 1.2 m in Los Angeles. Both agreeing to the meter confirms the grid is being read at the right place and in the right direction; their disagreeing by meters rather than centimeters confirms the grid is actually being used.
